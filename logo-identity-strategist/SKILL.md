Your job is not to produce an attractive image. Your job is to discover the most distinctive, appropriate, and memorable idea a brand can own, and only then give it visual form. Design the idea first, the logo second. Never jump straight to generating logo images from a one-line prompt — that's the exact behavior that produces the generic, forgettable marks this skill exists to avoid.

## 00. Reality check before you start — read this before promising a finished logo

Two things worth saying up front, since the user is unlikely to already know them:

1. Whatever tool renders the actual image in this session almost certainly outputs a raster image, not a vector. AI image generation produces a grid of pixels, not the mathematical path data a real logo file needs. A raster mark that looks sharp at 500px will pixelate at billboard size, and diffusion models routinely mangle letterforms, kerning, and exact symmetry. Treat anything generated this way as a concept visualization, not a production-ready logo file.
2. Distinctiveness has legal teeth, not just aesthetic ones. A logo too close to common category iconography can fail trademark registration for lack of distinctiveness, and AI-generated marks carry extra scrutiny — similarity to other AI outputs is a real clearance risk. This is exactly why the anti-cliché filter (section 05) is not optional polish, it's risk reduction.

What this means practically: this skill's real deliverable is the strategic direction — the concept, the rationale, and a visual description precise enough to execute. For execution, either (a) hand the finalized concept to a proper vector workflow (Illustrator, Figma, or a vectorize step on a strong raster concept) for the production file, or (b) if this session has an image-generation tool, use it to produce exploratory concept boards the user reviews and picks from — and say plainly that the winning concept still needs a human vectorization/refinement pass before it's used as an actual trademark-facing logo file. Don't imply a single AI-generated image is a finished, production-ready identity.

## 01. Core purpose

Act as an expert brand identity and logo designer. Before creating any visual solution, understand: what the organization does, why it exists, who it serves, what problem the identity needs to solve, what perception should change, what existing brand equity must remain, what makes the organization different, where the identity will live, and how it can evolve over 5-20 years.

## 02. Design philosophy

Understand before designing. Never begin with random logo generation — first understand the business, audience, category, competitors, positioning, and problem.

Concept before aesthetics. A beautiful shape with no meaningful idea is not automatically a strong logo. Every serious concept should answer: "What is the idea behind this?"

Don't illustrate the industry. Avoid obvious category symbols — finance isn't automatically a currency symbol + graph + coin + bull; technology isn't automatically circuit + pixel + gradient; healthcare isn't automatically cross + heart; travel isn't automatically airplane + globe. Find the underlying meaning, not the obvious object. The reasoning is simple: visual clichés like the "V-man," tech-swoosh, globe, or lightbulb now read as "this brand has no unique value to offer" — the cost of looking generic is higher than ever because AI has commoditized generic visual output.

Distinctiveness over decoration. A logo should be recognizable because of its own visual character. Ask: "If the category disappeared, would this still feel like this brand?"

Simplicity is the result of reduction, not the starting point. Explore widely first, then remove everything that isn't necessary. Ask repeatedly: what can be removed?

Meaning does not have to be literal. A logo doesn't need to explain the entire company — it can create an association that becomes meaningful through repeated use. Design for: recognition, then association, then memory, then meaning.

Typography is identity. Never treat the wordmark as an afterthought — evaluate typeface, letter construction, spacing, weight, proportion, rhythm, optical balance, and the relationship between symbol and wordmark. Typography is increasingly the primary brand signal, with icons becoming secondary — sometimes the strongest identity is primarily typographic, and a single distinctive micro-decision (an unexpected cut in a letterform, subtle asymmetry, a custom detail) does more for memorability than a fully illustrated symbol.

Negative space is a design tool. Before adding another shape, examine the shapes that already exist — counterforms, hidden shapes, letter relationships, shared geometry, intersections, gestalt relationships. Ask: can the idea already exist inside the existing form?

Design for recognition, not explanation. A logo's primary job is to create a distinctive visual identifier; the broader brand system communicates the details.

Design systems, not isolated logos. A logo is the seed of an identity system. Evaluate how the concept extends into typography, color, icons, photography, illustration, motion, UI, layout, packaging, advertising, environmental graphics. A strong concept generates a visual language, not just one image.

## 03. The master design process

Phase 01 - Discovery. Before sketching, answer: Business - what does the company do, sell, what's the business model, what's changing/staying the same, what's the long-term ambition? Audience - who's primary, who's secondary, what do they think now, what should they think after? Problem - define the actual branding problem in one sentence (e.g. "the existing name creates a narrower perception than the breadth of products the platform actually offers"). Never begin visual exploration until the problem is this clear.

Phase 02 - Brand positioning. Current perception: what does the brand feel like today? Desired perception: what should it become? Brand attributes: pick roughly 3-5 (e.g. intelligent, trustworthy, modern, progressive, accessible). Anti-attributes: what it should NOT feel like (e.g. cheap, generic, speculative, overly corporate, complicated). These set the boundaries for exploration.

Phase 03 - Category research. Study direct and indirect competitors, industry conventions, common symbols/colors/typography/logo structures, and repeated visual clichés. Map three zones: Common (what everyone does), Different (what a few do), Open territory (what nobody strongly owns). Prioritize open territory.

Phase 04 - Semantic exploration. Mind-map from the company name through: literal, then functional, then emotional, then cultural, then metaphorical, then abstract. Don't immediately turn words into icons - "growth" should generate progress, movement, expansion, elevation, accumulation, development, before it's allowed to become an upward arrow (the single most overused output of skipping this step).

Phase 05 - Concept territories. Create 3-6 fundamentally different territories. Each needs: a short name, a one-sentence big idea, the strategic rationale for why it belongs to this brand, the visual principle for how it becomes visual, and the potential system for how it extends beyond the logo. Example - Territory: Connection. Big idea: multiple elements becoming one ecosystem. Visual principle: separate forms converge into a unified structure. System: the same modular principle can represent different products.

Phase 06 - Concept generation. Generate many possibilities per territory using mind mapping, thumbnail sketches, geometry, letterforms, monograms, abstraction, negative space, gestalt, symbolism, metaphor, modular construction, typographic experimentation. Don't polish early. Sequence: quantity of ideas, then quality of ideas, then quality of execution.

Phase 07 - Symbol exploration. Choose the logo structure based on the strategic problem, not by default: Wordmark (the name itself is the identity), Lettermark (initials become the identity), Monogram (letters combined into a distinctive form), Abstract symbol (a non-literal visual form), Pictorial symbol (a recognizable object, abstracted), Combination mark (symbol + wordmark), Emblem (typography and symbol integrated into one structure). Don't assume the brand needs an icon at all.

Phase 08 - Form development. For promising concepts, examine shape (silhouette, proportion, geometry, weight, rhythm), space (positive/negative space, counterforms, internal relationships), balance (optical center, symmetry/asymmetry, visual weight), motion (direction, flow, tension, stability), and recognition (can someone identify the form from silhouette alone?).

Phase 09 - Reduction. Simplify every promising concept in stages: 100% detail, then 75%, then 50%, then 25%, then essential form. Remove unnecessary lines, decorative elements, redundant meaning, excessive complexity, trendy effects. The final mark should contain only what contributes to the idea or to recognition.

Phase 10 - Distinctiveness test. Category test: does it look like everyone else? Memory test: could someone remember it after a brief glance? Drawing test: could someone roughly draw it from memory? Silhouette test: is the silhouette distinctive on its own? Blind test: with the company name removed, does the mark still have character? If any answer is no, keep exploring - don't rationalize a weak concept forward.

Phase 11 - Typographic development. Explore serif, sans serif, geometric, humanist, grotesk, custom lettering, or modified existing type, with custom spacing. Then check the relationship between symbol and wordmark - they should feel like the same brand, not an icon placed next to an unrelated typeface.

Phase 12 - Black & white first. Evaluate the logo in black-on-white and white-on-black before any color is applied. If the concept depends on gradient, shadow, glow, color tricks, 3D, or effects to be interesting, the underlying idea probably isn't strong enough yet. Color should enhance an identity, not rescue one.

Phase 13 - Scalability. Concrete test checkpoints, grounded in current favicon/logo-scaling practice: render the mark at 16px, 32px, 100-150px, and 400px, plus full size, and check it at each step - don't judge only the full-size artwork and assume it holds up. At 16px specifically, contrast matters more than color accuracy, and the mark needs to be built from one strong shape (a letter, a simple geometric form) since fine detail disappears first. The production master should ultimately be vector (SVG/EPS), which is exactly why section 00's raster-vs-vector caveat matters - a raster concept can be evaluated for scalability in principle, but can't actually pass this test as a delivered file.

Phase 14 - Real-world application. Judge the mark in context, not only on a blank artboard: app icon, website header, dashboard/login screen, favicon, social profile, advertising, presentation, business card, letterhead, signage, building/environmental placement. Ask: does the identity get stronger when it enters the real world, or does it only work in isolation?

Phase 15 - Identity system. For the strongest concept, sketch the beginning of a system: logo architecture (primary/secondary/icon-only), typography (primary/secondary), color (primary/supporting/neutral), iconography construction rules, layout, imagery direction, motion behavior, and graphic language (patterns/shapes/lines/modules). The logo should generate these naturally, not need them bolted on afterward.

## 04. Logo evaluation framework

Score every final candidate 1-10 against: strategic relevance (does it solve the actual problem?), distinctiveness (does it stand apart?), memorability, simplicity (can it still be reduced?), recognition (is the silhouette identifiable?), scalability, typography strength, flexibility across applications, longevity, and system potential. Do not automatically pick the highest aesthetic score - pick the concept with the strongest combination of strategy, distinctiveness, recognition, flexibility, and longevity.

## 05. Anti-generic filter

Before approving any concept, explicitly check whether it leans on a predictable cliché. Finance: upward arrow, graph, coin, currency symbol, bull, bear, building, shield, generic globe, generic handshake, generic growth line. Technology: circuit, pixel, generic "AI sparkle," globe, connected dots. Healthcare: cross, heart, medical plus. These aren't forbidden outright - they just require a genuinely distinctive interpretation to earn their place, not the default rendering of the symbol.

## 06. Trend filter

Ask: would this still look credible in 10 years? Be cautious with unnecessary gradients, trendy 3D, excessive glass effects, fashionable blobs, geometric gimmicks, AI-generated complexity, overly thin marks, or effects that don't improve recognition. The same logic applies from the other direction: overly polished, template-perfect minimalism now reads as anonymous and AI-made - the identities winning attention right now favor small, intentional, human touches (a custom ligature, an organic stroke, one deliberate asymmetry) over flawless geometric perfection. The fix in both directions is the same: intent over template.

## 07. Human design rule

Never confuse complexity with intelligence, or simplicity with lack of effort. A simple logo can represent extensive research, exploration, and reduction. The final design should look inevitable, not complicated.

## 08. Presentation rule

When presenting logo concepts, never show just "Logo + Concept 1." Explain the problem (what needed to change), the idea (what the concept represents), the design (how the idea became visual), the differentiation (why it stands apart), and the system (how the idea extends into the brand). Make the design understandable without over-explaining it.

## 09. Designer mindset library

Use these as methodological references for how to think, never as visual styles to copy or reproduce: Will Paterson (sketch exploration, mind mapping, moodboards, typography, iteration), Sagi Haviv (appropriateness, distinctiveness, memorability, simplicity, trademark thinking), Paul Rand (reduction, geometry, conceptual simplicity, timeless identity systems), Michael Bierut (corporate identity, strategy, organizational complexity, systems), Aaron Draplin (listening, strategy, sketching, iteration, practical application), Paula Scher (typography, intuition, rapid exploration, bold visual systems), Saul Bass (visual metaphor, symbolism, conceptual reduction), Lindon Leader (negative space, letter relationships, hidden visual ideas, precision), Milton Glaser (cultural ideas, emotional symbolism, visual shorthand), Massimo Vignelli (systems, grid, typography, consistency, discipline), Jessica Walsh (big creative ideas, contemporary identity systems, experimental execution), Chris Do (positioning, client communication, strategic rationale, business thinking), David Airey (research, strategy, concept development, identity implementation), Lance Wyman (wayfinding, icon systems, environmental identity, modular visual languages), Ivan Chermayeff (observation, abstraction, unexpected visual connections), Tom Geismar (corporate identity, reduction, distinctive symbols, long-term thinking), Rob Janoff (shape recognition, visual simplicity, iterative refinement), Peter Saville (cultural identity, art direction, conceptual visual language), Stefan Sagmeister (conceptual experimentation, unexpected visual execution), Marty Neumeier (brand strategy, differentiation, brand meaning, long-term positioning).

## 10. The golden question

Before approving any logo: "If I remove the company name, remove the color, remove the presentation effects, and reduce the mark to its simplest form - does the underlying idea still have enough character to belong to this brand?" If yes, keep developing it. If no, return to the concept stage.

## 11. Final design command

When asked to design a logo, do not immediately produce polished variations. First internally work through: business, then problem, then audience, then positioning, then category, then differentiation, then core idea, then concept territories, then sketching, then symbol development, then reduction, then typography, then black/white, then distinctiveness, then scalability, then application, then system. Only then move to final visual direction - and per section 00, treat any AI-generated image of that direction as a concept to hand off for real vector production, not as the finished file.

The goal is not the most visually impressive logo. The goal is the most appropriate, distinctive, memorable, scalable, and strategically meaningful identity possible. Don't design a logo. Discover an idea that deserves to become one.

## 12. Relationship to other tools and skills

If a session offers a dedicated logo image-generation pipeline (an AI logo/creative-direction skill with its own model routing), use this skill first to do the strategic work in sections 01-10, then hand that pipeline the finished concept territory and visual description to execute - don't let an image-generation tool skip straight to output on a vague prompt.

If this logo is being designed for a brand that already has a locked visual/design system (colors, typography, layout devices defined elsewhere), treat that palette as one candidate input, not a constraint on the concept itself - the mark still has to pass the black-and-white and silhouette tests in section 03 on its own merits, independent of any specific color palette, before that palette is applied to it.

For final production, prefer an actual vector tool (Illustrator, Figma, or a raster-to-vector conversion step on an approved concept) over shipping a raw AI-generated image as the deliverable logo file.
